# Final Project Part III: More Visitors, More Money?

# The final data story

My final data story examines the relationship between national park visitation and visitor spending. It begins with an intuitive expectation: parks with more recreation visits generally have more total visitor spending. However, the story then shifts from total spending to estimated spending per recreation visit, showing that visitor counts alone do not capture how economically different park visits can be.

The final story uses three main visualizations to move through this argument. The first establishes the overall relationship between recreation visits and total visitor spending. The second changes the perspective to spending per recreation visit and reveals substantial differences across parks. The third explores length of stay as one possible clue for understanding why different types of park trips may be associated with different levels of spending.

The final takeaway is not that visitation is unimportant, but that it should not be used by itself to understand the economic contribution associated with national park tourism. When evaluating the economic contribution of national parks, spending per visit and trip characteristics can provide useful context alongside total visitation.

# Changes made since Part II

The main goal for Part III was not to add more visualizations, but to make the existing story more focused and precise. User research in Part II showed that readers generally understood the central argument and found the spending-per-recreation-visit comparison useful. At the same time, the feedback identified several places where the explanatory section could be clearer.

One change involved the length-of-stay section. In the Part II version, the term "LodgeOut" appeared without a clear definition, and feedback showed that this terminology was confusing. In the final version, I replaced this technical term with the more accessible phrase "visitors staying overnight outside the park." I also added context explaining that I compared a small group of parks with different visitation and spending-per-visit patterns. These changes make the comparison easier to understand without requiring readers to be familiar with National Park Service terminology.

I also narrowed the section that asks what might explain differences in spending per visit. The Part II prototype mentioned length of stay, visitor origin, and spending patterns as possible characteristics to explore, but only length of stay was actually analyzed in the story. Rather than adding additional visualizations simply to cover every possibility, I revised the final version to focus on the evidence that is actually presented: length of stay. This keeps the narrative more focused and avoids creating expectations for analyses that are not included.

Another important change was strengthening the conclusion. The earlier version primarily ended with the takeaway that visitor counts do not tell the whole economic story. For the final version, I made the next step more explicit by asking readers not to rely on visitation counts alone when evaluating the economic contribution associated with national parks. Instead, the conclusion encourages readers to consider spending per visit and trip characteristics alongside total visitation.

Throughout these revisions, I also kept the language around length of stay cautious. The story presents length of stay as one possible clue rather than claiming that longer stays cause higher spending per visit. This distinction was important because the data in this project shows associations and comparisons, not a causal relationship.

## The audience

The intended audience for this story is a general audience interested in national parks, tourism, and the economic relationship between parks and nearby communities. The story is particularly designed for readers who may initially assume that the most heavily visited national parks necessarily tell the largest economic story.

I did not assume that readers would have prior knowledge of National Park Service visitor spending data or economic analysis. This influenced both the structure and language of the final story. Instead of beginning with a technical measure, the story starts with the familiar idea of visitor counts and total spending. It then introduces spending per recreation visit as a different way of looking at the same parks.

The Part II user research helped refine this approach. The three participants generally understood the main argument, but their feedback showed that unfamiliar terminology and unexplained transitions could make the later part of the story harder to follow. For the final version, I therefore simplified technical language, narrowed the explanatory section to the evidence actually shown, and made the final recommendation more explicit.

## Final design decisions

One of the main design decisions was to preserve the overall narrative progression developed in Part II:

**visitation → total visitor spending → spending per visit → length of stay → takeaway and action**

I kept this structure because each visualization has a specific role in the argument. The first visualization establishes the expected positive relationship between visitation and total spending. The second changes the unit of comparison and creates the main surprise of the story by showing substantial differences in spending per recreation visit. The final visualization then introduces length of stay as one example of how park trips themselves can differ.

I also decided not to add additional visualizations for visitor origin or other spending patterns. Although these could be interesting directions for future analysis, adding them would have expanded the scope of the story without necessarily making the central argument clearer. Based on the Part II feedback, I decided that improving the explanation of the existing evidence was more important than increasing the amount of information presented.

Shorthand remained the final presentation format because its scrolling structure allowed me to control the sequence in which readers encounter the evidence. The visualizations are not intended to function as independent charts placed next to unrelated text. Instead, each section creates a question or expectation that the following visualization helps address.

For the final conclusion, I used a visually prominent statement to separate the call to action from the preceding explanation. This makes the final recommendation clear: visitation is useful, but it should be considered alongside spending per visit and characteristics of the trip when interpreting the economic story around national parks.

# Final thoughts

The most important lesson I learned from this project is that adding more data does not necessarily make a data story stronger. After Part II, there were several possible directions for expanding the analysis, including visitor origin and additional spending patterns. However, the user research showed that the more important issue was making the existing argument clearer and more precise.

This changed how I approached the final revision. Instead of adding more visualizations, I focused on simplifying terminology, making transitions clearer, narrowing the explanatory section, and distinguishing between what the data demonstrates and what it only suggests. I also learned that a conclusion and a call to action serve different purposes. A takeaway tells the reader what the analysis shows, while a call to action makes clearer how the reader should use that information.

If I continued developing the project, I would consider adding multiple years of data to examine whether the spending-per-visit patterns remain consistent over time. I would also be interested in exploring regional differences or additional trip characteristics. For this final version, however, I chose to keep the scope focused on the central argument: visitor counts are important, but they do not tell the whole economic story.
